12   Abstract
13   Floor-to-stand ability predicts adverse outcomes, and floor-rise practice can improve task
14   performance, but neither finding identifies the mechanism of any benefit. We propose the
15   sitting pause between descent and ascent as an experimental probe that separates three
16   processes in functional aging: elastic energy stored during descent, fresh muscle-generated
17   preload before ascent, and chronic adaptation to changing support geometry. A falsifiable
18   mechanical prediction is stated in advance. If the returnable viscoelastic branch has an
19   effective relaxation time of at most 10 s, consistent with human tendon hysteresis and muscle
20   stress-relaxation measurements, then descent-attributable return should fall to no more than
21   13.5% of the prompt-reversal value after a 10 s pause and 1.8% after 20 s; measured retention
22   above 25% after a 15 s pause would reject that band. An illustrative viscoelastic balance,
23   extended with a tension-only variant that removes unphysical compression artefacts, makes
24   the arithmetic explicit. We propose sequential identification experiments with motion-gated
25   pauses and an assessor-blinded 12–16 week trial of floor practice against dose-matched
26   chair/step practice, with a pre-committed usefulness criterion: a between-group difference in
27   usual gait speed of at least 0.10 m/s. Mutual information enters only as a secondary held-out
28   predictive test, and no thermodynamic bound is applied to the task budget. Prespecified
29   findings distinguish passive recycling, muscle–tendon coordination, task learning, and
30   general functional transfer; disability-free survival and mortality require independent
31   investigation.
32   Keywords: functional aging; floor-to-stand transitions; elastic recoil; postural control;
33   sensorimotor adaptation; healthspan

34   1. Introduction
35   The ability to move between the floor and standing integrates mobility, strength, balance, and
36   the selection of a feasible movement strategy. Baseline sitting–rising test performance
37   predicts mortality in clinical cohorts, but predictive ability does not demonstrate that
38   repeatedly performing the task improves survival (de Brito et al., 2014; Araújo et al., 2025).
39   A useful longevity hypothesis must identify a modifiable process and distinguish it from the
40   health that already permits floor rising. Exercise has a causal benchmark in functional aging:
41   structured physical activity reduced major mobility disability in the LIFE randomized trial
42   (Pahor et al., 2014). A floor-specific proposal therefore requires evidence of incremental
43   benefit or a measurable practical advantage over suitable activity.
44   Floor practice is also an established research topic. Early strategy training improved floor-rise
45   ability, and recent trials have examined functional and psychological outcomes (Hofmeyer et
46   al., 2002; Seeley et al., 2026). The 2026 Floor-Hugging exploratory study combined floor
47   exposure and post-fall recovery practice, with favorable functional changes in a small
48   uncontrolled sample (Ghai, 2026). Consequently, neither recommending floor practice nor
49   linking it to balance constitutes the contribution of the present paper.
50   The unresolved question addressed here concerns the relationship between an acute energy-
51   recycling mechanism and a chronic training mechanism. Lowering the body may load elastic
52   tissues. Returning promptly to standing could recover some of that energy, whereas a
53   sustained sitting interval changes the loading history and allows some stored energy to
54   dissipate or become inaccessible. However, muscles can load elastic tissues afresh before
55   standing, and repeated transfers may train coordination independently of passive return. The
56   same observable improvement can therefore have different sources and implications.


                                                  Page 2
57   We propose using the sitting pause as an experimental discriminator, together with
58   independent control of rising speed and measured support. Our central hypothesis is that
59   repeated floor transitions can preserve adaptive control across changing body-support
60   configurations, with transfer beyond the practiced task, even when energy inherited from the
61   preceding descent is reduced. This is a hypothesis of functional aging. Its relevance to
62   longevity proceeds through sustained mobility and independence and requires separate
63   outcome evidence.

64   2. What prior work establishes, and what remains open
65   2.1. Resting posture, transitions, and prognosis are different exposures
66   The inactivity mismatch hypothesis arose from observations of nonambulatory postures
67   among Hadza adults and measurements showing elevated muscle activity in some squatting
68   postures compared with chair sitting (Raichlen et al., 2020). It concerns muscular
69   engagement during rest. Static floor sitting, squatting, kneeling, the number of transitions,
70   and their timing should therefore be recorded separately. Their effects cannot be inferred
71   from a common label such as floor use.
72   Similarly, a sitting–rising test score describes capacity under an assessment protocol, not
73   habitual exposure. Illness, weakness, pain, or limited mobility can both reduce floor use and
74   increase subsequent disability or mortality. A baseline association can arise without an
75   independent training effect. Claims that any population performs a particular number of rises
76   each day require direct exposure measurement, and demographic differences across birth
77   cohorts discourage treating long-lived populations as natural exposure experiments (Poulain
78   and Herm, 2024).
79   2.2. Elastic return and movement economy are already connected
80   A passive knee-assist device stored energy during sitting and released it during standing,
81   reducing metabolic power in repeated chair transfers in healthy adults (Lee et al., 2020). This
82   demonstrates an engineering route to economy, not the magnitude of native floor-rise recoil.
83   Ultrasound during squat jumps without a preceding countermovement also demonstrated
84   elastic loading through muscle action (Kurokawa et al., 2001). Thus, observing tendon recoil
85   does not establish recovery of energy from the preceding descent.
86   Tendon measurements depend on tissue, operating range, and protocol. Human experiments
87   show that measured hysteresis can depend on force-relaxation duration, and loading-rate
88   experiments do not support assigning a universal loss fraction (Peltonen et al., 2013;
89   Sasajima et al., 2023). Aging-related tendon mechanics have been associated with walking
90   energetics, while training studies demonstrate load-dependent mechanical adaptation (Eriksen
91   et al., 2019; Gray et al., 2024). None identifies ordinary floor transitions as an adequate
92   tendon-training stimulus.
93   2.3. Stability and economy must be interpreted together
94   The energetic cost of balancing is established. Manipulating sway during standing produced a
95   nonlinear relation between sway and metabolic cost (Houdijk et al., 2015). Recent work in
96   this journal further showed that reduced efficiency in older adults can represent a strategy that
97   prioritizes equilibrium, rather than a loss of the ability to plan economical movement
98   (Mathieu et al., 2026). Increased activation can therefore be functional compensation;
99   reduced activation can reflect either improved coordination or inadequate stabilization.


                                                  Page 3
100   Our framework does not claim a new general energy–stability trade-off. It asks whether a
101   specific exposure produces transferable adaptation after accounting for the preceding
102   movement’s elastic history. The proposed contribution is the combined test of pause-
103   dependent recoil, source attribution, and chronic transfer under an active comparator. Broader
104   complexity-loss theories of aging already connect adaptive capacity with physiological
105   dynamics (Lipsitz and Goldberger, 1992). The present hypothesis does not use increased
106   signal entropy as a substitute for demonstrating useful function.

107   3. The hypothesis: acute recoil and chronic adaptation can dissociate
108   Three mechanisms are separated in Table 1. First, descent can store recoverable elastic
109   energy. Second, muscles can generate new elastic preload immediately before or during
110   ascent. Third, repeated transitions can alter strength, mobility, and the control of force as the
111   body changes contact and support. These processes interact, but none is interchangeable with
112   the others.
113   The primary proposition is that floor-transition practice can produce a useful
114   improvement in untrained function beyond dose-matched chair/step practice, and that
115   any general adaptation need not depend on substantial descent-stored elastic return
116   during a delayed rise. Its mechanism is proposed to include practice of estimating body state
117   and coordinating force across varied support configurations. General exercise, strength
118   adaptation, familiarity, and reduced concern about the floor remain competing explanations.
119   This proposition makes the distinction between an efficient repetition and an effective
120   training exposure consequential. An immediate reversal may reduce muscular demand
121   through energy return but also reduce the stimulus per repetition. A delayed rise may require
122   more fresh muscular work without necessarily imposing a better training stimulus. The
123   direction and magnitude of chronic effects cannot be read directly from acute economy.
124   Neither maximal recoil nor maximal energy expenditure is the proposed objective.

125   4. A thermodynamic account of the pause
126   4.1. Mechanical energy and metabolic cost
127   For a defined mechanical system, let K denote kinetic energy, U g gravitational potential
128   energy, U e recoverable elastic energy, P a signed muscle mechanical power, P o other external
129   mechanical power, and D passive dissipation. The balance is
                                 d
130                                 ( K +U g +U e )=P a + P o − D , D ≥ 0 .(1)
                                 dt
131   Elastic return is represented by a decrease in U e and is not added as a separate source. An
132   ascent raises gravitational potential by approximately m g Δ h; the corresponding descent
133   reverses that change. Over identical initial and final mechanical states, net potential-energy
134   change is zero. Physiological costs remain because force generation, stabilization, negative-
135   work control, and recovery consume chemical resources. In particular, negative mechanical
136   muscle power during descent does not imply negative metabolic expenditure. Recoil
137   redistributes mechanical work within Equation 1; it neither reverses entropy production nor
138   converts joules into years of life, and no entropy term enters the task budget.

148   The relaxing branch loses stored energy during the pause, whereas the equilibrium branch can
149   retain it. The model therefore predicts neither complete disappearance of recoil after a
150   universal interval nor full retention. Real tissue loading changes during sitting, and energy
151   may cease to be accessible to the subsequent movement even when it remains stored locally.
152   A seated hold is also different from unloading duration; changing rising speed changes both
153   mechanical and muscular conditions.
154   Equation 4 supports a prespecified, falsifiable band. In vivo work on human tendon shows
155   that hysteresis becomes insensitive to relaxation time beyond roughly 1 s, with the resolvable
156   dependence confined to shorter intervals (Sasajima et al., 2023), and stress-relaxation studies
157   of human muscle and soft tissue report multi-second relaxation behaviour in which most
158   resolvable force decay occurs within tens of seconds (McHugh et al., 1992; Van Loocke et
159   al., 2008). Taking an effective relaxation time no larger than 10 s for the returnable branch,
160   Equation 4 bounds retention of descent-stored return at 13.5% after a 10 s pause, 4.9% after
161   15 s, and 1.8% after 20 s. The prespecified prediction is therefore that descent-attributable
162   return after a 15 s pause lies below 5% of the prompt-reversal value in at least the tissues
163   where descent loading is demonstrable. A measured retention above 25% after a 15 s pause
164   — implying an effective relaxation time above 22 s — would reject the band for the
165   returnable branch and redirect the hypothesis toward fresh preload and adaptation. Because
166   tissue-specific effective values in floor rising are unmeasured, the band is stated for the
167   tissues and conditions actually tested, and it constrains the pause probe rather than the
168   training claim.
169   Supplementary Material S1 provides an analytic implementation checked against independent
170   numerical integration over 225 normalized cases, plus a matched tension-only variant in
171   which unloading stops at the slack point. Its purpose is verification of the balance, not
172   physiological estimation. The signed linear law permits compression force, and 58 of its
173   histories show negative net unloading return; the tension-only variant removes all such cases,
174   confirming that they were compression artefacts of the signed law rather than consequences
175   of viscoelasticity. Translation still requires measured preload, nonlinear properties where
176   appropriate, and task geometry. No human relaxation time, recoil fraction, metabolic saving,
177   or survival effect has been fitted. The standard material law is established mechanics; novelty
178   resides in the proposed biological comparison rather than in these equations.

179   5. Information as a secondary predictive analysis
180   Maintaining and changing posture requires information about orientation, motion, contact,
181   and available support. Controlled human perturbation experiments provide an established
182   approach to estimating sensory contributions to corrective responses (Peterka, 2002). Within
183   our hypothesis, floor transitions repeatedly challenge state estimation and force selection as
184   contacts change. Whether this exposure preserves control better than comparable exercise is
185   an empirical question, and it is answered with measured control performance, not with an
186   information quantity substituted for it.
187   For a discrete state X and observation Y , mutual information is

189   These definitions use natural logarithms and therefore nats (Shannon, 1948). They do not
190   automatically describe useful control: random noise can increase signal entropy, and common
191   task phase can create dependence without a new physiological mechanism. In this study
192   design, mutual information enters at exactly one point — the secondary analysis of §6.3 — as
193   a held-out predictive measure of how well baseline sensors and movement features predict
194   future transition difficulty, compared against age, strength, and usual gait speed. It is a
195   statistic about prediction, not a mechanism, and it is not inserted into Equation 1 as an energy
196   source or saving.
197 The reason for restraint is quantitative. The physical cost floor for information erasure,
198 Q b a t h ≥ k B T ln 2 per bit in contact with a reservoir at temperature T , is about 4 × 10− 21 J at
199 body temperature; Landauer’s principle and its controlled tests concern this physical memory
200 operation (Landauer, 1961; Bérut et al., 2012). That bound is six to seven orders of
201 magnitude below the mechanical work of a single floor transition and far more below its
202 metabolic cost. No thermodynamic bound of this kind constrains the task budget of Equation
203 1. Established results on work extraction with measurement and feedback (Sagawa and Ueda,
204 2010; Horowitz and Esposito, 2014), and biological precedents for energy–accuracy trade-
205 offs in defined signaling networks (Lan et al., 2012; Still et al., 2012; Sartori et al., 2014),
206 apply to specified physical controllers; whole-body movement has not been mapped onto
207 them, and this paper does not assert such a mapping. The hypothesis concerns preservation of
208 useful state estimation and action, measured directly.

209   6. Experimental identification before a training claim
210   6.1. Measure the actual exposure
211   A wearable and sampled-observation method should first distinguish floor sitting, squatting,
212   kneeling, chair sitting, and transitions. Validation requires independently annotated examples,
213   count agreement, support-use errors, and assessment of wear-time gaps. Record sitting
214   duration, pause before ascent, movement strategy, cadence, other exercise, and background
215   health. The study should recruit across customary floor use rather than assigning exposure by
216   nationality. Static holding costs and transfer costs must remain separate.
217   6.2. Acute comparison of mechanical histories
218   The first mechanistic experiment compares descent followed by prompt reversal, a brief
219   pause, and a longer supported sitting interval drawn from measured habitual exposure. A rise


                                                      Page 6
220   from an already seated state and a chair-rise comparison help examine new muscular preload.
221   Order is randomized, rest is standardized, and two tolerable rising durations are tested
222   independently of pause if feasible. No pause duration is selected as a universal physiological
223   cutoff, and no ballistic movement is necessary.
224   Before dynamic transfer measurements, selected-tendon force–deformation measurements
225   require repeatability under controlled loading. During transfers, synchronize kinematics,
226   muscle–tendon ultrasound, activation and relevant support forces. A foot force plate alone
227   can omit hand, knee, or seat loading. Moment-arm, antagonist, and deformation uncertainties
228   must be propagated. Task endpoints and support conditions should be matched where feasible
229   and deviations reported.
230   Tissue work is more directly identifiable than its origin. Force–deformation data alone cannot
231   separate energy retained from descent from simultaneous muscular input. The attribution
232   model must follow the tissue’s loading history, include measured preload where possible, and
233   state its assumptions. If attribution remains uncertain, report bounds and total tissue work
234   rather than a precise passive fraction. Failure to demonstrate meaningful descent-derived
235   return after a sitting interval rejects that explanation for the interval; it does not establish
236   absence of newly generated elastic loading.
237   Metabolic measurements require repeatable bouts and defined recovery observation, with
238   holding energy included when pause durations differ. Breath-by-breath oxygen use cannot
239   precisely assign energy to one brief rise or isolate the cost of sensing from actuation. A pause
240   effect on rise time alone is therefore insufficient evidence of elastic energy recovery.
241   6.3. Chronic comparison and functional transfer
242   Following exposure and feasibility validation, an individually randomized, assessor-blinded
243   12–16-week study would compare tolerable floor-transition practice with calibrated
244   chair/step practice. Select the main dose-matching variable before efficacy testing, such as
245   net bout metabolic energy, and match contact, schedule and duration. Measure positive
246   mechanical work and joint-level loading separately. Equal metabolic dose cannot perfectly
247   match strength stimulus, movement complexity, and tissue strain; residual differences limit
248   mechanistic attribution and must be reported.
249   The primary outcome is a baseline-adjusted between-group difference in standardized usual
250   gait speed, an untrained task. Floor-rise ability and speed are secondary because they are
251   directly practiced. Perturbation recovery, support needs, strength, power, mobility and life-
252   space provide distinct secondary outcomes. An initial planning anchor of 0.05 m/s has been
253   proposed for small meaningful gait-speed change, but the final between-group target and
254   variance must be justified for the population (Perera et al., 2006). Sample size, missing-data
255   handling and useful-effect thresholds are frozen before enrollment. A feasibility sample is not
256   a powered test of efficacy.
257   Post-training mechanistic assessment repeats the acute pause conditions. The key dissociation
258   is a functional advantage that remains in delayed-rise conditions after a measured reduction
259   in descent-stored return, together with benefit on untrained function. This would challenge
260   the necessity of passive recycling as the explanation, but would not alone isolate
261   informational adaptation from strength or mobility changes. A subsequent nonfloor
262   coordination comparator, such as calibrated stepping and turning practice, can test whether
263   any advantage is specific to floor geometry or reflects generic coordination training.

264   Dose tolerability, supported strategies, symptoms, missed sessions and adverse events are
265   recorded. Participant selection must not restrict enrollment to exceptionally capable older
266   adults and then generalize to frailty. Any human investigation requires finalized ethical
267   approval and prospective registration where applicable. These studies are proposed and have
268   not been conducted.

269   7. Predictions and rejection criteria
270   Table 2 specifies the comparisons that can challenge the hypothesis. Three observations must
271   not be conflated: evidence for energy return, evidence for improvement on the practiced task,
272   and evidence for transfer beyond it. A pause-related decline in measured return supports a
273   mechanical component under the tested conditions. A chronic improvement restricted to
274   prompt reversal can be consistent with acute assistance or task learning and does not establish
275   general reserve. Improvement on untrained gait and recovery after delayed rises is the more
276   consequential functional result.
277   The floor-specific proposition is weakened or rejected if the confidence interval excludes the
278   prespecified useful benefit over an appropriately implemented active comparator, with
279   adequate exposure separation. A wide interval is inconclusive. If differences are limited to
280   the practiced transfer, the evidence supports learning that task. If a nonfloor coordination
281   program reproduces the advantage, floor geometry is not necessary to that benefit. These
282   outcomes should constrain the claim rather than prompt post hoc redefinition of dose or
283   mechanism.
284   For secondary information analysis, define the input or future task state, observation history,
285   prediction interval, estimator and covariates in advance. Use held-out trials and surrogates
286   that preserve relevant autocorrelation. Establish incremental predictive value beyond ordinary
287   force, kinematics and movement history. Neither an increase in entropy nor lagged
288   dependence alone identifies causal neural information flow, thermodynamic dissipation, or
289   successful adaptation. Failure of this added analysis does not become evidence for an
290   unmeasured information field.

291   8. Implications for healthspan and longevity
292   The proposed pathway is through preservation of capacities needed for independent activity.
293   Greater capacity relative to daily task demands could reduce fatigue, permit continued
294   participation, and improve recovery from disturbances. It is also possible that a convenient
295   routine improves adherence without possessing a floor-specific mechanism. These practical
296   effects should be measured rather than inferred from the theoretical account.
297   A later cohort must measure habitual exposure prospectively and repeatedly, account for
298   baseline and time-varying health, and distinguish disability-free survival from mortality. Diet,
299   social context, socioeconomic conditions, other activity and illness can confound exposure–
300   outcome associations. Birth and death records require validation, and analyses must address
301   delayed entry, loss to follow-up and illness that changes movement before an outcome. A
302   sitting–rising hazard ratio cannot be converted into years gained per rise. A biomarker change
303   or an aging-clock estimate cannot replace the required clinical outcome.
304   The current paper therefore predicts a potential contribution to functional healthspan, not a
305   demonstrated extension of lifespan. It also offers no molecular cascade from generic
306   mechanosensing to Klotho, YAP or senescence markers. Such work would require a defined
307   tissue, signal, chemical budget and independent mediation analysis.

308   9. Discussion
309   The sitting pause exposes a tension in the original recoil intuition: the movement may be
310   trainable and clinically useful even when energy from the preceding descent contributes little
311   to the eventual rise. A measured dissociation would direct attention from passive recycling
312   alone to the coordination and adaptation required to reorganize a loaded body. Conversely,
313   finding that benefits are confined to immediate reversal would delimit the relevance of recoil
314   for everyday floor use. Both outcomes are informative.
315   Several elements of this framework are established: floor-rise training, active resting
316   postures, muscle–tendon energy redistribution, sensory contributions to posture, energy–
317   stability trade-offs, and information-theoretic limits in physical systems. The proposed
318   contribution is their operational separation through pause history, source attribution, and
319   chronic transfer testing in functional aging. A targeted comparison with current primary
320   literature did not identify the complete proposed experimental package; this bounded search
321   does not establish priority or justify describing the framework as the first of its kind. An
322   independently replicated functional benefit, rather than a new label, would establish its value.
323   The main limitations are lack of human parameter calibration, difficulty identifying the
324   source of tissue work, imperfect dose matching, possible ceiling effects, and selection against
325   severe limitation. The illustrative material model is linear and does not solve whole-body
326   movement. Information measures remain proxies unless a physical controller model is
327   identified. The proposed trial cannot establish survival or isolate every mediator. These limits
328   define the next measurements needed rather than being resolved by theoretical terminology.
329   The resulting research question is specific: can repeated floor transitions improve untrained
330   function beyond comparable activity while any benefit persists after descent-stored recoil is
331   reduced? Answering it would clarify whether the useful component lies in energy recycling,
332   new muscular loading, learned coordination, or their combination. This is a falsifiable route
333   from biological countercurvature to functional aging, with longevity retained as a
334   downstream question requiring its own evidence.
